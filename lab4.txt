Answer written questions here:
1. Starting from a low iteration count means each frame finishes quickly. Every pixel's
mandelbrotSmooth call has to return before the frame can be shown, so a low maxIters gives the
user a rough image right away and keeps panning and zooming responsive. Detail is then refined over
the next frames (by doubling) only while the view stays still, instead of freezing on one expensive
3200-iteration frame every time the view changes.

2. Since each pixel is independent, split the image into tiles (e.g. blocks of rows) and have a
coordinator hand each tile, along with the world bounds and maxIters, to a pool of worker threads,
cores, or machines. The coordinator waits for all of them to finish and writes their results into the
buffer. Handing tiles out dynamically from a queue balances the load, since tiles near the set's
boundary/interior take many more iterations than tiles far outside it.

3. Visually, averaging 4 sub-pixel samples is supersampling anti-aliasing: it smooths the jaggies
along the set's boundary and turns the speckled noise in high-frequency regions into a smoother
average colour that better reflects what the pixel covers. For performance, it needs 4x as many
mandelbrotSmooth calls per frame, so rendering takes roughly 4 times longer (plus a small cost for
averaging), which hurts responsiveness unless it is only applied once the view has stopped moving
or is combined with parallelism.
